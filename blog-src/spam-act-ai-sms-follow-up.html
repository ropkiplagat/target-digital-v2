Spam Act rules for AI text message follow-up in Australia

Published 6 October 2026 · ACMA pages read on 5 and 6 October 2026 · By Target Digital · 8 minute read

The Spam Act applies to AI text message follow-up in the same way as to a text typed by a person: you need consent, you must identify your business, and every marketing message needs a working way to unsubscribe. A system that sends texts for you on autopilot repeats any mistake in those three rules thousands of times.

This guide sets out the rules from ACMA's own guidance, what changed for branded text messages in July 2026, and how to build an AI follow-up so it stays inside them. It is general information, not legal advice. Calls are a separate regime, covered in our AI outbound calling compliance guide.

Is your follow-up text covered by the Spam Act?

ACMA's guidance on avoiding spam applies to marketing messages and emails. A text that promotes your services is marketing, and a follow-up that adds an offer, a pitch or an upsell is more likely to be treated that way. The OAIC says a partial exemption applies to certain messages, such as messages of a factual nature only, without a commercial element, so a plain booking confirmation is treated differently from a promotion. We have not assumed any particular text is exempt, because a message with promotional content is commercial, so take advice before relying on an exemption. ACMA's release on Lululemon says that if an electronic message contains any promotional or sales content, it is considered commercial regardless of whether the message has any other purpose, so we keep service texts free of sales material. Treat any follow-up text that promotes your business as a marketing message, and take advice if you want to rely on an exception.

The Spam Act also covers texts sent for you. ACMA says that even if someone else sends your marketing messages, you must still have consent from each person who will receive them. Using an AI platform does not move that duty.

What the Spam Act requires of an AI text follow-up

ACMA sets out three steps: get consent, identify yourself, and make it easy to unsubscribe.

1. Consent

There are two types, express and inferred.

  • Express consent means the person knows and accepts they will receive marketing messages from you. ACMA calls this best practice. People can give it by filling in a form, ticking a box on a website, over the phone or face to face.
  • Inferred consent is possible where the person has knowingly and directly given their number and it is reasonable to believe they would expect marketing from your business, usually through a provable, ongoing relationship where the marketing relates directly to it. ACMA says inferred consent is not as reliable as express consent and does not cover messages sent after someone has just bought something.

Two points matter for automation. First, you cannot send a text to ask for consent, because ACMA says that would itself be a marketing message. Second, the burden is on you: keep a record of who gave consent, when and how, because under the Spam Act it is up to you to prove it.

A lead who submits an enquiry form has asked about your service. Whether that gives you consent to send promotional follow-ups is the sort of argument you do not want to have. A tick box on the form, covered in our guide to consent from a lead form, removes it.

2. Identify yourself

ACMA says the message must accurately identify your name or business name and include correct contact details. If someone else sends it for you, the message must still identify you as the business that authorised it, using your legal name or your name and ABN. This information must stay correct for at least 30 days after you send the message.

3. Unsubscribe

ACMA says every commercial message must contain an unsubscribe option that:

  • presents the instructions clearly;
  • honours a request within five working days;
  • does not require a fee, or cost more than the usual amount, such as a standard text charge;
  • works for at least 30 days after you send the message;
  • does not require extra personal information, a login or an account.

ACMA's SMS examples are "Reply STOP" and "Unsub:" followed by a 1800 number. Its tip matters for branded sender IDs: alphanumeric message headers are generally not capable of receiving return messages. If you send from a branded name, put a phone number or link in the text.

The 2026 change: registered sender IDs

From 1 July 2026, texts sent with a business name at the top, called a sender ID, must have that sender ID registered. ACMA says an unregistered sender ID is replaced with "Unverified", and all "Unverified" texts are grouped in the same thread on a person's phone, with scam messages. You register through your telco or message provider. A sender ID must be two to eleven characters and must match your registered business name, company name, trademark or domain name if you have an ABN.

If you send from a phone number, or do not use a sender ID, ACMA says you do not need to do anything. That has a side benefit: a normal number can receive "STOP" replies. If your follow-up texts arrive as "Unverified", the sender ID is the likely cause, so ask your provider.

What regulators do with breaches

ACMA publishes its penalties. In March 2026 it said Lululemon paid $702,900 for sending more than 370,000 emails with commercial content that had no unsubscribe option, and in April 2026 that Latitude Finance paid $3.96 million after it was found to have breached the spam laws more than 2.7 million times. Those are large businesses, but the pattern is the one to notice: automation turns a single missing line into hundreds of thousands of breaches.

Building an AI text follow-up that stays inside the rules

  1. Check consent before every send. The system should read a consent flag, not assume one, and send nothing to a lead without it.
  2. Put identification in the template. Business name and a working contact detail in every message.
  3. Make STOP work everywhere. Treat a "STOP" reply as an opt-out across texts, and it is our recommendation to also stop calls to that number. Honour it faster than five working days: automation can do it at once.
  4. Use a number that can receive replies, or include a free number or link.
  5. Register your sender ID if you use one, through your message provider.
  6. Log everything: the consent wording, time and form, each message sent, and each opt-out.

Calls and texts are separate regimes

The Do Not Call Register's own description is that it lets people opt out of most unsolicited telemarketing calls and faxes, and ACMA places marketing texts and emails under the Spam Act. In practice that means a lead's Do Not Call Register status does not decide whether you may text them, and consent to a text is not automatically consent to a call. A consent box on your form should cover both channels in plain words. Our guides to calling leads on the Do Not Call Register and consent to call from a lead form cover the call side.

What to store as your consent record

ACMA says to keep a record of who gave consent, when and how, and that it is up to you to prove it. For an AI follow-up system, that translates into a record stored with each lead:

  • the person's name and phone number;
  • the exact consent wording they saw, and the version of the form;
  • the date and time they gave it, and the channel: a form, a tick box, a phone call or face to face, which are the methods ACMA lists for express consent;
  • every message the system sent, with its time and template;
  • any opt-out, with its time and how it was received.

A record that lives only inside your AI provider's dashboard is a risk if you change provider. Ask for an export.

An example text and where each rule sits

This is an illustration of how the three rules can fit into one short follow-up. It is our draft, not ACMA's wording, and you should adapt it with your adviser:

"Hi Sam, this is Smith Plumbing (ABN [your ABN]) about the quote you asked for. We will call tomorrow at 9am. Reply STOP to opt out, or call [your number]."

  • Consent: Sam ticked a box on the form that covered texts, and your system checked it before sending.
  • Identification: the business name appears, with an ABN and a working contact number, and ACMA says this must remain correct for at least 30 days.
  • Unsubscribe: a clear "Reply STOP" instruction, which ACMA gives as an example, sent from a number that can receive replies.

The text is also confined to the purpose of the enquiry. ACMA's Lululemon release makes the point that a message containing promotional content is commercial however it started, so a service message that sneaks in an offer is a marketing message.

Sender ID or phone number: the trade-off

ACMA says a sender ID helps customers trust that a text came from you and makes it harder for scammers to impersonate your business. It also says that if you send from a phone number you do not need to register anything, and that its alphanumeric-header tip applies: a branded name generally cannot receive a reply. That sets up a real choice, and the answer depends on how you want people to opt out.

Marketing lists and outsourced sending

ACMA says that even if someone else sends your messages, you must still have consent from each person who will receive them, and that if you buy or use a marketing list you are still responsible for consent. If a provider's platform sends your texts, put these questions to it:

  1. Does the system check a consent flag before every message, and does it stop if the flag is missing?
  2. Does every template carry our legal name or name and ABN, and a working opt-out?
  3. When someone replies STOP, how quickly is the opt-out applied, and is it shared across every channel the system uses?
  4. Who registers the sender ID, and what happens to our messages if registration lapses?
  5. Can we export the consent records and message logs at any time?

Spam Act: common questions

Do I need consent to text leads under the Spam Act?

For marketing messages, yes. It can be express or inferred, and ACMA recommends express. You must be able to show it.

Can I text a lead to ask them to opt in?

No. ACMA says you cannot send an electronic message to ask for consent, because that is a marketing message. Collect it on the form, on the phone or face to face.

What changed in SMS rules in 2026?

From 1 July 2026, branded sender IDs must be registered or the text is labelled "Unverified". Texts from ordinary phone numbers are not affected.

How fast must I honour an unsubscribe?

Within five working days under ACMA's guidance. With an automated system there is no reason to wait.
