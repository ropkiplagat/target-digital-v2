Speed to lead: why the first 5 minutes decide the sale

Published 5 October 2026 · By Target Digital · 9 minute read

Speed to lead is the time between someone enquiring and your first attempt to contact them. For most Australian service businesses it is the cheapest conversion lever there is, and the one least often pulled. You have already paid for the lead through ads, SEO or a referral. The only question left is how long it sits before a person picks up the phone.

This guide covers what the research says, where it falls short, how to get under five minutes without new software, and when automation is worth it. It also covers the Australian rules you need to check before calling someone back.

What speed to lead means, and how to measure it

Three timestamps matter, and the Lead Response Management study defines them well:

  • Lead created: in the study, the moment a person submits a web form. We apply the same idea to any enquiry that reaches you, such as a call you miss or a message.
  • First dial attempt: when someone first tries to reach them.
  • First contact: the first real conversation.

Your speed to lead is the gap between the first and second timestamps. Not the time to read the enquiry, and not the time to send an email. The time until a person is trying to speak to them. If you cannot say that number in minutes right now, that is the first thing to fix.

What the research says about speed to lead

The study most people quote comes from Lead Response Management, working with Professor Oldroyd of MIT and a dataset from InsideSales.com. It covers three years of data from six companies, more than 15,000 web-generated leads and more than 100,000 call attempts. As published in the study's own write-up:

  • The odds of making contact fall more than tenfold within the first hour, and the odds of qualifying a lead fall more than sixfold.
  • The odds of qualifying a lead fall fourfold between a 5-minute and a 10-minute response.
  • Responding in 5 minutes rather than 30 is associated with a 21-fold difference in the odds of qualifying a lead. The write-up also cites a 100-fold difference in contact ratios.
  • Response time mattered more than the day of the week or the time of day, though Wednesday and Thursday, and late afternoon, performed best for contact.
  • After about 20 hours, extra call attempts stopped helping and appeared to hurt the chance of reaching the lead.

What the study does not tell you

The figures come from Lead Response Management's own write-up, and its site also sells lead distribution software, so read it as an interested party. Treat the multiples as direction, not a forecast for your business. The dataset comes from a sales-software vendor, it covers six companies whose location the page does not state, and it covers leads from web forms that were then called. We could not find an Australian equivalent, and a plumber taking a phone enquiry is not the same as a software company calling a demo request. The size of the effect for you will differ. The direction, that waiting makes everything harder, is well supported and matches common sense: the person enquired while their problem was in front of them, and they rarely enquire with only one business.

We have deliberately left out some widely repeated statistics, such as average response times measured in hours or days, because we could not open the original sources to check them. If a number cannot be traced, it should not carry an argument.

Why most small businesses miss the five-minute window

It is rarely laziness. It is the shape of the work:

  • The enquiry arrives while you are on a roof, in a consult room or driving between jobs.
  • Enquiries land in an inbox or a form notification that is checked twice a day.
  • Nobody owns the job. Everyone assumes someone else saw it.
  • Weekends and evenings, when many people enquire, have no cover at all.

None of that is fixed by a better ad. It is fixed by deciding who responds, how, and how fast.

How to hit a five-minute speed to lead target manually

You can get most of the benefit with a process, before you buy anything.

  1. Make delivery instant. Every new enquiry should trigger a notification to a phone, not just an email to a shared inbox.
  2. Name one owner per shift. One person, whose only job on a new lead is to call within five minutes.
  3. Call first, then text. If the call goes unanswered, send a short message straight away so the conversation stays open: who you are, that you saw their enquiry, and when you will try again.
  4. Set an after-hours rule. Decide in advance what happens to a 9pm Saturday enquiry. Either someone covers it, or the lead gets an immediate acknowledgement and a call at a stated time.
  5. Measure the median weekly. Time from lead created to first dial attempt. The median, because one slow Friday should not hide a good week.
  6. Change channel after a day. If calls are not connecting after many hours, the study's finding that more attempts can hurt is a reason to switch to a message rather than keep dialling.

Calling a lead back in Australia: what to check first

Speed does not remove the rules. This is general information, not legal advice, and you should take advice for your own situation.

  • Consent comes first. Whether you can call or message someone who has just enquired depends on what they agreed to when they did. Capture a clear consent on the form or call, and keep a record of it.
  • The Do Not Call Register and the Spam Act are separate regimes. The register is about telemarketing calls. The Spam Act covers commercial SMS and email, including a working unsubscribe.
  • ACMA enforces these rules. It has recently announced multi-million dollar penalties against large businesses for telemarketing and spam breaches.

Start with ACMA's pages on telemarketing and e-marketing and its consumer consent expectations, and the text of the Do Not Call Register Act 2006. What slow speed to lead costs you

Here is the arithmetic, with made-up numbers to show the method, not results anyone should expect. Suppose you get 40 enquiries a month, you currently have a real conversation with 40% of them, and one in four conversations becomes a job worth $3,000.

  • Today: 40 × 40% = 16 conversations, 4 jobs, $12,000.
  • If faster follow-up lifted contact to 60%: 40 × 60% = 24 conversations, 6 jobs, $18,000.

Speed to lead: common questions

What is a good speed to lead?

Under five minutes is the common target, and the research above supports it as a direction. Within the first hour is the minimum worth aiming for. Beyond that, the study found contact becomes much harder.

How fast should you call a new lead?

As soon as you can, ideally within five minutes of the enquiry. If you miss, send a short message straight away and try again at a time you state.

Does speed to lead matter more than lead quality?

They multiply each other. A fast call to a poor lead wastes the speed, and a good lead called two days later wastes the lead. If you have to choose where to start, response time is usually the cheaper one to fix.

Can I improve speed to lead without software?

Yes. An owner per shift, instant phone notifications and a weekly median will get many businesses most of the way. Automation matters when enquiries arrive faster, or at more hours, than people can cover.
